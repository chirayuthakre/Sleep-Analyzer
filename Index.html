06 — background

Why timing, not just duration

Sleep is paced by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), a small hypothalamic clock that runs on a roughly 24-hour cycle and is reset daily mainly by light hitting the retina. The SCN governs the nightly rise of melatonin, which normally begins a couple of hours before habitual bedtime and signals the body that night has arrived. Keeping bedtime and wake time at similar clock positions night after night keeps this melatonin rhythm anchored; shifting them around forces the clock to keep re-adjusting, which is part of why irregular schedules feel worse than short-but-consistent ones.

Social jetlag is the mismatch between the sleep schedule your body clock wants and the one imposed by work, school, or weekend social life — commonly estimated as the gap between the midpoint of sleep on free days and on workdays. Larger gaps are associated with poorer mood, alertness, and metabolic markers, in much the same way that crossing time zones produces jetlag, except the "time zones" here are self-imposed by a Friday-night-late, Monday-morning-early routine.

Evening light — including screens — is the other lever: light in the blue-enriched range suppresses melatonin release most effectively, so exposure close to bedtime can push the clock later and delay sleep onset, independent of how sleepy a person otherwise feels. That is the mechanism this log's screen-time comparison is probing.

refs (general): Roenneberg, Allebrandt, Merrow & Vetter, "Social Jetlag and Obesity," Current Biology (2012) · Czeisler et al. on melatonin and light suppression, various · Duffy & Czeisler on circadian phase and health, Sleep Medicine Clinics
